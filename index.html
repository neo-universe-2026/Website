        The Sonificator, our audio engine, samples each video frame on a coarse grid and writes the soundtrack on the fly: nothing is prerecorded, and the music starts when the video does. This is how it maps the picture to sound today.

        Colour becomes energy
        The engine looks for coloured pixels of any hue (black, white and grey are ignored) and measures how strong they are. More colour means more musical energy: louder layers, a faster tempo between 68 and 96 beats per minute, and a denser groove.
        Energy has three states
        Low energy gives Sparse Drift: slow bass notes under a soft pad. Medium energy gives Flowing Groove: a syncopated bass and busier chords. High energy gives Intense Climax: driving bass, full chords and a faster melody.
        Position becomes space and register
        The centre of the coloured area steers the music. Left of centre pans to the left speaker, right of centre to the right. Colour low in the picture lifts the melody an octave, colour high in the picture sits in the base register, and in between lifts it a fifth.
        Spread becomes arpeggio
        Closely clustered colour keeps a sparse arpeggio. Widely scattered colour fills the arpeggio in up to every sixteenth note, with occasional octave jumps when it spreads even farther.
        Warmth becomes timbre
        How red versus blue the coloured areas are shifts the melody between warm second-harmonic tones and cooler, brighter ones.
        The video picks its key
        The first strongly coloured frame sets a musical fingerprint: its hue and horizontal position choose one of twelve keys and one of three chord progressions. Everything plays in a major pentatonic scale, so no note ever clashes with the pad.
        Movement drives monochrome
        For videos with almost no colour, such as white currents on dark ocean, the engine falls back on differences between consecutive frames: moving brightness still drives the energy, pan and spread.

        One honest limit: a browser only lets the engine read the pixels of videos served from this site, so the site fetches and transcodes NASA's videos into its own videos/ folder at build time. If you replace a video with a link to another domain, the engine can't read its frames and the soundtrack stays quiet. The meter under each dataset shows the engine's readings while a video plays.

        The challenge

        NASA's Earth Information Center makes striking visualizations of our changing planet: temperatures, ocean currents, forests, storms. Pictures alone leave out blind and low-vision people. The Earth Information Jukebox challenge asks teams to turn these visuals into sound, so everyone can explore the data.

        Our approach

        We start from NASA's own videos. Our Sonificator engine, written in Rust and compiled to WebAssembly, samples every frame, measures where colour, brightness and movement sit, and renders a pentatonic soundtrack live: colour energy sets the tempo and loudness, position sets the stereo pan and pitch register, spread sets the rhythm density, and the first strong frame sets the key. This site puts every dataset on a globe you can spin, and every page works with a screen reader and the keyboard.
